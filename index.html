04 / The advanced workflow

Type a fucking sentence.

No secret prompts. No “Act as a world-class Harvard-educated McKinsey CEO, my grandma will die if you fail.” Describe what you need like you’re asking a smart friend who has zero context about your life. Because it has zero context about your life.

Help me write an email to [who] about [the thing]. I want them to [the outcome I need]. Tone: [polite / firm / “per my last email”]. Under 120 words. No corporate filler, and don’t start with “I hope this email finds you well.” Here’s the context: [paste the thread or my notes].

Fill in the brackets. Paste the actual context.

That’s a prompt. You can now unfollow the guy selling “1,000 ChatGPT Prompts That Will 10× Your Life” for $49. He’s filming in a rented Lambo. It’s not his.

05 / Give it something to work with

It can’t read your
fucking mind.

You typed “make it better” and expected magic. Better how, Sherlock? Shorter? Nicer? Less likely to get you fired? You wouldn’t walk into a doctor’s office and just say “ow.”

−Vague request: make this email better

+Useful request: Rewrite this email to my landlord about the broken heater. Firm but polite. Mention I first reported it on March 3. Under 120 words.

−Vague request: am i dying

+Useful request: Headache behind my left eye for 3 days, worse at night. I sleep 5 hours and drink 4 coffees. Likely causes? What signs mean I should see a doctor today?

−Vague request: fix it. no mistakes.

+Useful request: The menu overlaps the logo on phones. Fix the mobile layout, keep desktop the same, check both.

Tell it what you want, what you’ve got, what to leave alone, and what “done” looks like. Give it the actual thing — the email thread, the PDF, the full error message — not a blurry photo of your monitor taken at a 45° angle with your thumb in it.

And if it asks you a clarifying question, answer it. That’s not it being difficult. That’s it already being better at this than Dave.

07 / The objection department

Yes, but—
oh my fucking god.

Pick the thing you were about to say.

“But I don’t want to bother it.”+

It’s a computer, you sweet summer child. It has no calendar. It doesn’t sigh. It won’t leave you on read, and it will never once say “per my last message.”

You bother Dave eleven times a day, and Dave has a family. Ask the robot the same dumb question forty times. It’ll answer the fortieth like it’s the first, and it won’t bring it up at lunch.

“But it makes stuff up.”+

Sometimes, yes, with the confidence of a man explaining your own job to you. So does your uncle, and you still let him do crypto at Christmas dinner.

Ask for sources. Click them. Double-check anything involving money, medicine, law, or your boss. “It sounded really sure” is not evidence. It wasn’t for your uncle either.

“But it’s not a doctor.”+

Correct! Neither is the guy on TikTok, and you bought his sea moss.

Use it to translate the jargon, prep questions for your seven-minute appointment, and figure out whether this is “drink water and sleep” or “go get that looked at.” Don’t use it for dosing decisions or instead of the actual doctor. And if it’s chest pain, trouble breathing, or anything that feels like an emergency: close this tab and call emergency services. That part isn’t a bit.

“But I’ll get dumber.”+

You haven’t memorized a phone number since 2009, and you can’t find north without a blue dot. That ship has sailed, Magellan.

Use it to learn, not to skip learning. Ask it to explain, then quiz you, then grade your answer. Make it the annoying tutor, not the kid who lets you copy their homework.

“But it’ll take my job.”+

It just recommended a restaurant that closed in 2019. With total confidence. Your job is fine.

The real threat is the coworker who asks it things, finishes by four, and goes home. That guy is fucking dangerous.

“But which one is the best?”+

The one you already have open. Seriously. There’ll be a new “best” one next Tuesday, announced with a chart that has no y-axis.

You’re writing an email, not picking a religion.

“But my stuff is private.”+

Fair, and good instinct. Don’t paste passwords, your company’s secret sauce, or your entire medical history into whatever has a shiny input box. Check what your work allows, and use that.

(Concern noted from the person who gave their face to an aging-filter app in 2019.)

“But it writes like a LinkedIn post.”+

Then tell it not to. “Write like a tired human. Short sentences. No ‘delve.’ No ‘I hope this finds you well.’ No ‘It’s not just X — it’s Y.’” It’ll listen. Mostly.

It’s not the agent’s fault you never mentioned you hate fun.

“But I tried it once and it sucked.”+

You typed “write something good” in 2023 and got something mid. Garbage in, LinkedIn post out.

Try a real task with real context. If it still wastes your time, stop using it. You don’t have to defend a chatbot on the internet. Incredible perk of being an adult.

“But I like doing things myself.”+

Then do it yourself. Seriously. Enjoying the work is a perfectly good reason.

This page is for the person on their ninth tab of “how to write a resignation letter that sounds chill.” If you’re happily doing the thing, you’ve already beaten this website.

Delegate the typing. Keep the judgment. “My agent said so” is the new “I saw it on Facebook.” Nobody respected that one either.
